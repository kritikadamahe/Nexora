% ============================================================================
% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Demand forecasters for perishable goods are usually given event information (holidays, promotions, benefit days) taken from a clean, after-the-fact calendar. At ordering time that information can be missing, late, false or mislabelled. We study when event signals reduce inventory cost and how a forecaster should be trained when its signals are imperfect. Four forecasters share one replenishment policy: one ignores events (A), one is trained on the true calendar (B), one on the signals as they were available at ordering time (C), and one on the true calendar with simulated signal errors injected at estimated rates (D). Arm D needs no archive of past announcements, which few retailers keep. In simulation, accurate event signals cut cost by 36.8\%. With false-alarm-heavy signals, clean-calendar training costs 16.1\% more than ignoring events, while C and D are 17.0\% and 18.1\% cheaper than B; D and C are statistically indistinguishable. When D's assumed false-alarm rate differs from the true one, overestimating it remained cheaper than ignoring events in every cell of the sweep, whereas underestimating it cost up to 37\% more. A sweep over event strength shows that all effects scale with how strongly events move demand. On Walmart M5 food sales, where SNAP benefit days lift sales by about 17\%, event signals cut cost by 5.3\% and both C and D protect against false alarms, with sizes close to those the simulation predicts for that strength. On Favorita perishables, where holidays lift sales by about 9\%, no difference is distinguishable from zero.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
perishable inventory, demand forecasting, event signals, signal quality, noise injection, quantile forecasting, simulation.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

% ============================================================================
% I. INTRODUCTION
% ============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Perishable retail has a narrow margin for error: over-ordering turns into waste and under-ordering turns into lost sales \cite{nahmias1982perishable}. Event information is a natural input to demand forecasts, but event features are usually built from a clean calendar that is known with certainty after the fact. In deployment the signal is different. Events are announced late, some are never announced, some announcements prove false, and labels can be wrong. A model that learned to trust a clean calendar may react strongly to a signal that is wrong.

Training on the signals exactly as they were available at ordering time avoids that mismatch, but it requires an archive of every past announcement, including those that later proved wrong, and few retailers keep one. A retailer does have the clean calendar and can estimate how often its event feed errs. This paper asks three questions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does training on imperfect signals, either as they were available (arm C) or simulated from the clean calendar (arm D), reduce the cost harm of imperfect signals compared with clean-calendar training (arm B)?
    \item How wrong can the error rate assumed for arm D be before its benefit disappears?
    \item How do the answers depend on how strongly events move demand, and do they hold on real sales data?
\end{enumerate}
Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A controlled simulation of perishable replenishment in which signal quality (misses, late announcements, false alarms, mislabelling) is a tunable input and forecasters are judged by inventory cost rather than forecast error alone (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
    \item A noise-injection training recipe (arm D) that needs only the clean calendar and an estimated error rate, and a train/serve mismatch sweep showing that overestimating the false-alarm rate is safe while underestimating it is costly.
    \item A map of where event signals help, do nothing or hurt, over event strength and false-alarm rate.
    \item Two real-data cases with clustered intervals: Walmart M5 food sales with strong scheduled events, and Favorita perishables with weak ones.
\end{itemize}
The claims concern operational cost under one heuristic replenishment policy, and we make no claim about deployed systems.

% ============================================================================
% II. RELATED WORK
% ============================================================================
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Perishable inventory control.} Fixed-shelf-life inventory has a long theoretical tradition \cite{nahmias1982perishable}. Optimal policies are hard to compute because the state must record the age profile of the stock, which has motivated near-myopic and heuristic policies \cite{nandakumar1993near}. Broekmeulen and van Donselaar \cite{broekmeulen2009heuristic} give a heuristic for the retail case with batch ordering, positive lead times and time-varying demand, and Haijema and Minner \cite{haijema2019improved} quantify the value of using stock-age information when ordering. Reviews of deteriorating-inventory models cover the wider literature \cite{bakker2012review, janssen2016literature}. We do not propose a new policy. We fix a simple order-up-to rule with first-in-first-out issuing and use it as a common yardstick for the forecasts.

\textbf{Calendar, event and promotion effects in retail forecasting.} Holidays, promotions and other special days are among the main drivers of retail demand, and their treatment is a recurring topic in reviews of retail forecasting \cite{fildes2022retail}. Promotional forecasting has been studied with regression-type models \cite{trapero2015identification}, including for perishable items, where promotional uplift interacts with waste \cite{vandonselaar2016analysis}. Huber and Stuckenschmidt \cite{huber2020daily} compare machine-learning forecasters for daily retail demand with a focus on calendric special days. Public benchmarks supply event calendars as features: the Favorita data include a holiday calendar \cite{favorita2018kaggle}, and the M5 data include calendar events and SNAP days \cite{makridakis2022m5background}. In M5, methods built on gradient-boosted trees \cite{friedman2001gbm, ke2017lightgbm} performed strongly \cite{makridakis2022m5}. External signals such as weather have also been shown to carry operational value \cite{steinker2017value}. In these studies the event inputs are usually treated as known and correct; how errors in them affect results is rarely examined.

\textbf{Forecast quality judged by inventory outcomes.} Several authors argue that forecasts for replenishment should be judged by the inventory performance they produce, not only by accuracy. Gardner \cite{gardner1990evaluating} evaluates forecasting methods through inventory trade-off curves, and Kourentzes et al. \cite{kourentzes2020optimising} show that standard accuracy measures can be poorly aligned with inventory outcomes and propose optimising forecasting models for inventory planning. A related line connects forecasting and decisions directly. Quantile regression \cite{koenker1978regression} gives the critical-fractile quantile that a newsvendor order requires, and feature-based or data-driven newsvendor models learn order quantities from covariates \cite{ban2019big, huber2019data, bertsimas2020predictive}. Our forecasters follow this pattern: a gradient-boosted quantile model sets the order-up-to level, and the arms are compared by simulated cost.

\textbf{Imperfect inputs and train--serve mismatch.} When the distribution of inputs at deployment differs from that seen in training, a model fitted on one can perform poorly on the other; this is the general problem of dataset shift \cite{quinonero2009dataset}. A clean, after-the-fact event calendar used for training but not available at ordering time is closely related to data leakage, in which a model is built with information that will not exist when predictions are made \cite{kaufman2012leakage}. Learning using privileged information \cite{vapnik2009new} studies the opposite design choice, deliberately exploiting information that is present only during training. Two remedies for noisy inputs are also relevant. Adding noise to inputs during training acts as a regulariser \cite{bishop1995training}, and data augmentation for time series is an active area \cite{wen2021time}. Our arm D, trained on the clean calendar with simulated signal errors injected at estimated rates, is an instance of this idea applied to event features.

\textbf{Positioning.} This paper combines these threads in one setting. Rather than asking whether event features improve accuracy, we ask how the quality of the event signal available at ordering time changes the cost of a perishable replenishment policy. We compare training on the clean calendar, on the signals as they would have been available, and on the clean calendar with injected noise, and we vary the error rates used in training and in serving separately. We also vary event strength independently of signal quality, so that results are reported as a map of regimes rather than a single effect size. To our knowledge, the effect of imperfect event signals on perishable inventory cost, and the choice of training signal under such errors, has received little attention in the literature reviewed above.

% III. METHODOLOGY
% ============================================================================
\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Replenishment Problem}
One SKU is managed daily with lead time $L = 2$ days and shelf life $m = 4$ days. Stock is sold first-in-first-out, unmet demand is lost, and units reaching the end of their shelf life expire. Costs are 0.05 per unit-day held, 1.0 per lost unit and 0.6 per expired unit. Each day, after the delivery is received and before demand is served, an order $q$ is placed by an outdating-corrected order-up-to rule,
\begin{equation}
    q = \max\bigl(0,\; S - (\mathrm{IP} - \mathrm{E}_{L+1})\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{IP}$ is the inventory position, $\mathrm{E}_{L+1}$ is the projected FIFO expiry over the next $L+1$ days, and the order-up-to level $S$ is the $\tau$-quantile of the forecast of demand over days $t,\dots,t+L$. The rule is a heuristic and is not claimed to be optimal. All arms share it.

\subsection{Event Signals}
An announcement is a tuple $(\text{announce day}, \text{start}, \text{duration}, \text{type})$. On ordering day $t$ a forecaster sees only announcements with announce day $\le t$. The error process has four parts: missed events (probability $p_{\text{miss}}$), announcements delayed to the event's start day ($p_{\text{late}}$), spurious events at rate $\lambda$ per day, and spurious events labelled as the highest-uplift type ($p_{\text{mis}}$). Three signal conditions are used (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}); on-time announcements arrive 14 days ahead.

\subsection{Forecasting Arms}
All arms use pooled gradient-boosted quantile regression (scikit-learn \texttt{HistGradientBoostingRegressor} with quantile loss \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}), one model per quantile level, with the cumulative demand over days $t,\dots,t+L$ as target. Features use only demand before $t$, scaled by the trailing 28-day mean: SKU identifier, weekday, 7- and 28-day means, a seasonal-naive window and volatility. The event arms add, for each event type, the number of signalled days in the window.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A (blind):} no event features.
    \item \textbf{B (clean-trained):} trained on the true calendar, every event announced 14 days ahead, then served the imperfect signal.
    \item \textbf{C (as-available-trained):} trained on the training-period part of the same signal realisation it is served with. This needs an archive of past announcements.
    \item \textbf{D (noise-injected):} trained on the true calendar corrupted by an independent draw of the error process at an assumed error rate, then served the imperfect signal. This needs only the clean calendar and an estimate of the error rates. In the main experiments the assumed rates equal the true ones; Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} varies them.
\end{itemize}
Under clean signals B, C and D see identical features and must give identical results, which serves as a built-in consistency check. The quantile $\tau$ is chosen separately for each arm and condition by validation-period simulated cost, and the test period is evaluated once.

\subsection{Demand Data}
\textbf{Synthetic.} Daily demand is negative-binomial with a mean equal to a SKU base level times a weekday profile, a linear trend and an event multiplier. Event uplifts vary by type and SKU, and 20\% of SKUs do not respond to events. Each seed is an independent world with 40 SKUs over 730 days. \emph{Event strength} scales all event log-uplifts, with 1.0 the original simulation (mean log-uplift 0.8).

\textbf{Walmart M5.} The three Wisconsin stores $\times$ the 33 food items that sold on at least 85\% of days in every store (99 series), over 753 days from 2014-05-01 to 2016-05-22 \cite{makridakis2022m5background}. Event types are SNAP benefit days, which are scheduled and published in advance (125 occurrences, 250 days, 30 occurrences in the test period), national or religious events (30 occurrences), and cultural or sporting events (11 occurrences). Training-period mean log-uplifts are $+0.157$ for SNAP days (about $+17\%$), $-0.133$ for national or religious events and $+0.004$ for cultural or sporting events, which places SNAP days at roughly strength 0.2 on the synthetic scale. M5 items are not labelled perishable; the same shelf life is imposed on them, so M5 tests how event strength moves cost rather than the spoilage of specific products. Zero-sale days are 4.3\% of the panel.

\textbf{Favorita.} Five Quito stores $\times$ 20 perishable items (100 series: 80 produce, 10 meats, 5 dairy, 5 poultry) over 742 days from 2015-08-01 to 2017-08-15 \cite{favorita2018kaggle}. Events come from the holiday calendar: national holidays (21 occurrences, 5 in the test period, mean log-uplift $+0.087$, about $+9\%$ and roughly strength 0.1), national events (6 occurrences) and local holidays (2 occurrences, none in test). The April 2016 earthquake is excluded because it could not have been announced. Zero-sale days are 0.4\% of the panel.

On both real data sets demand is fixed recorded sales, so seeds vary only the signal errors and the model fits, and recorded sales are censored by stockouts. Training, validation and test periods end on days 430, 574 and the last day; after a 7-day warm-up, 170 (M5) and 159 (Favorita) test days are evaluated, against 161 in the synthetic worlds.

\subsection{Statistics}
In the synthetic experiments each seed is an independent world, so the unit of analysis is the seed. The primary evidence is the per-seed 95\% $t$-interval of relative cost differences and an exact two-sided sign-flip test. A comparison is called supported when the interval excludes 0 and its Holm-adjusted $p$ \cite{holm1979simple} is below 0.05, with the correction applied across all 15 comparisons that are not identical by design. Bootstrap intervals over (seed, SKU) units \cite{efron1993bootstrap} are computed but ignore dependence and are not used for conclusions.

On real data every seed shares the same demand, so seeds are not independent. There we cluster by product (33 items for M5, 20 for Favorita) and report the per-cluster $t$-interval and the sign-flip test with Holm's correction across the 13 distinct comparisons in each data set. With more than 20 clusters the sign-flip $p$ is estimated from 200{,}000 random sign patterns instead of all $2^n$. Store-level clustering is also computed but has only 3 (M5) or 5 (Favorita) clusters, too few for any test to reach significance.

\subsection{Discussion}
\textbf{Answers to the three questions.} First, training on imperfect signals removes most of the cost harm that false alarms do to a clean-trained model, in simulation ($-17\%$ to $-18\%$ against B) and on M5 ($-1.3\%$ to $-1.7\%$), and noise injection (D) does this as well as an archive of real announcements (C). Second, D is robust to overestimating the false-alarm rate but not to underestimating it. Third, the size of every effect is governed by event strength: when events move demand a lot, accurate signals are worth tens of percent and false alarms can cost as much, while when they move demand by under about 10\%, as in Favorita, the whole question is worth little.

\textbf{Practical reading.} Estimate how much events move demand before investing in event features or feed quality. If the uplift is small, a blind forecaster is as good. If it is large, use event features, and train on the clean calendar with simulated errors at a deliberately generous false-alarm rate; this needs no announcement archive and avoids doing worse than a blind forecaster when the feed is unreliable.

\textbf{Scope.} The Favorita result concerns everyday perishables, mostly produce, around national and local holidays, and the M5 result concerns food sales around SNAP days with an imposed shelf life. Neither should be read as a statement about all retail events.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simulated signal errors.} On real data, as in simulation, the imperfect signals are generated from the true calendar by our error process, because no archive of real-time announcements was available. C and D are therefore compared under errors that follow the process D assumes.
    \item \textbf{One mismatch dimension.} The mismatch sweep varies only the false-alarm rate; misses, delays and mislabelling were held at their nominal values.
    \item \textbf{One policy and one cost structure.} All arms share one heuristic order-up-to rule, with lead time 2, shelf life 4 and fixed cost ratios. Other policies or cost ratios could change the sizes of the differences.
    \item \textbf{Simplified operations.} A single SKU at a single echelon, with no capacity limits, ordering costs, substitution or supplier variability.
    \item \textbf{Censored demand and imposed shelf life.} Real sales understate demand during stockouts (suspected stockouts are 2.3\% of M5 and 0.4\% of Favorita series-days), and M5 items are given a shelf life they may not have.
    \item \textbf{Few clusters.} Real-data inference treats items as independent; with 3 (M5) or 5 (Favorita) stores, store-level clustering cannot support any conclusion. Twenty synthetic seeds is a modest number, and the strength and mismatch sweeps use 10 seeds with uncorrected $p$-values.
    \item \textbf{Library-version sensitivity.} Results are deterministic in a fixed environment but change with the scikit-learn version. Every reported result was produced from committed code in the public repository, in one pinned environment recorded with each run.
    \item \textbf{No pre-registration.} The hypotheses were set during prototype design, and arm D was added after the first results.
\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================================
% V. CONCLUSION
% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Event signals are worth about as much as events move demand, and imperfect signals can destroy that value. In simulation, accurate event information cut inventory cost by 37\%, and false-alarm-heavy signals made a clean-trained model 16\% worse than ignoring events. Training on the clean calendar with simulated errors recovered that loss as well as training on an archive of real announcements, and stayed better than ignoring events whenever the false-alarm rate was overestimated. On Walmart M5 food sales, where SNAP days lift sales by about 17\%, event signals cut cost by 5\% and both remedies protected against false alarms, with sizes close to the simulation's prediction; on Favorita perishables, where holidays lift sales by about 9\%, none of it mattered. A natural next step is a data set with a recorded archive of real-time announcements, so that real signal errors replace simulated ones. Code, configurations and saved results are available at \url{https://github.com/kritikadamahe/Nexora/tree/main/research/event_signal_inventory}.
